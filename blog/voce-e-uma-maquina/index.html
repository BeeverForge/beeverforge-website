Are you a machine?The future of software developers in the age of AI

For decades, programmers were paid to turn other people’s work into software. Now their own work is a ticket in the queue.

Roberto Costa Silva PereiraBeeverForge Journal

The first line got cheap

On a task board, “Add a delete-account button” sounds simple. AI can deliver the button before the team agrees on what “delete” means. Does the person lose access immediately? Does their data disappear too? The answer fits on one line. The consequences stay in the product.

In No Silver Bullet[1], Frederick Brooks located the difficulty of building software in deciding what it should do. Decades later, Robert C. Martin, known as Uncle Bob, told Aviator[2] that working with agents led him to pay closer attention to architecture and system behavior. When code arrives fast, the questions the team left unresolved also reach the people using the product faster.

Will the company still need me?

It’s an uncomfortable question because, for some changes, it’s already possible to picture a ticket moving from the queue to a proposed change without passing through a developer’s hands. In the open-source BeeverForge project, formerly OpenFactory[3], agents implement a task, run checks, and submit the change for review. Whoever decides what goes into production still needs to understand what they’re approving. That person might come from another department.

So will AI replace software developers? In 2025, the ILO and Poland’s NASK institute[4] estimated that about one in four workers worldwide held a job with some exposure to generative AI. Change often starts with a task. What that does to a job comes down to a practical decision. Who takes on the work that remains, and how much time do they get to do it?

The missing hour

In Atlas of AI[5], Kate Crawford follows the labor, energy, and materials behind an answer that appears in seconds. Now look at the other side of the screen. If a team gets ten changes to review in the time it used to get two, code production has sped up. On the review side, it has gained a backlog.

You’re a machine

In Capital, Marx describes what happens when machinery sets the pace of work. In Discipline and Punish, Foucault shows how schedules and assessments teach people to keep pace. Bruna Brito and I brought that question to social media in an essay for MIT Technology Review Brasil[6]. Today it applies just as well to the task board. When the dashboard only counts completed tickets, who wants to be the person who asks for one more day to understand the problem?

“You’re a machine” always sounded like a compliment for someone who could do it all. Now imagine the machine takes a day to do what used to take a week. On Monday afternoon, do you close your laptop or get four more days of work?
