Are you a machine?The future of software developers in the age of AI

Will AI replace software developers? Agents already write, test, and review code. That can reduce manual work on some changes and reshape what developers do. The effect on jobs depends on which tasks companies automate and how they organize the work that follows.

The central question

As execution speeds up, deciding what to build, who answers for the outcome, and how to use the time automation frees up becomes more consequential.

When productivity becomes praise

“You’re a machine.” We tend to say it after someone has done it all. They delivered quickly, dealt with the unexpected, and kept up the pace. The compliment reveals how we measure their work. What happens to that measure when machines start doing some of the work themselves?

The industrial factory made the pace of production a measure of performance. In Discipline and Punish, Michel Foucault describes how discipline governs time, space, and movement. In Capital, Karl Marx examines how machinery absorbs tasks and sets the pace for workers. Digital work is also divided into stages and outputs that can be measured and, in some cases, automated.

In an essay for MIT Technology Review Brasil[6], Bruna Brito and I looked at how social networks shape behavior. AI brings the same question to the work of the people who built many of those tools.

The hard part comes before the code

Imagine a ticket on a task board: “Allow booking cancellations.” An agent can implement the change and run the tests. Who can cancel? How late? What happens to the payment? The answers affect everyone who uses or operates the system.

Frederick Brooks addressed this difficulty in No Silver Bullet[1]. Defining how software should behave and verifying that it does what people need requires understanding the problem. Code generation speeds up one part of that work.

In an Aviator interview[2], Robert C. Martin described how working with agents led him to focus on architecture and try the software himself to understand what needed to change. His experience shows how work can shift from writing every instruction to making decisions about product behavior.

What AI can already take on in software development

An approved task can go to an agent for implementation, pass through checks, and arrive as a proposed change for review. The open-source BeeverForge project, formerly OpenFactory[3], brings these stages into one workflow. For some changes, people can set the goal and accept the result without a developer guiding every technical step.

That possibility can alter what teams do and how large they are. In 2025, the ILO and Poland’s NASK institute[4] estimated that about one in four workers worldwide held an occupation with some exposure to generative AI. The estimate concerns the potential to automate tasks. The authors expect occupations to change, while the effect on job numbers depends on how organizations adopt the technology.

The work beyond the screen

In Atlas of AI[5], Kate Crawford traces the workers, data, energy, and materials that make these systems possible. Work moves around inside a company, too. Time freed from implementation can go toward investigating problems, reviewing changes, or operating infrastructure. It can also become pressure to approve more changes in a day.

Return to the booking. If the change triggers a refund that shouldn’t have happened, someone has to spot the error, respond to the people affected, and fix the process. The number of lines the agent wrote tells us little about that work. What a company chooses to recognize when it says “you’re a machine” helps determine what work will be valued when producing code gets faster.
