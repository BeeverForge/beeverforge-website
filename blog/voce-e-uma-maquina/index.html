Are you a machine?The future of software developers in the age of AI

Imagine your team’s AI has learned to cancel bookings. The code passes every test. On its first morning in production, a customer gets two refunds. The report calls the release a success. Whose mistake was it?

The test that passed

The ticket was one line long: “Allow booking cancellations.” The customer canceled in the app. The payment status took a while to update, so she called support. A support rep canceled the same booking from their dashboard. Each route had its own test, and both passed. Nobody had tested what would happen when the two routes met.

In No Silver Bullet[1], Frederick Brooks draws attention to the work of figuring out what software should do. Here, the rule only becomes obvious when the customer switches from the app to the phone. A booking can be canceled through two channels, but it should only be refunded once. Someone who knows how customers use support may spot that before someone who knows the code.

Does a developer need to be in the loop?

The problem becomes another ticket, this time with the rule spelled out. An agent implements the fix, runs the tests, and submits the proposed change for review. The open-source BeeverForge project, formerly OpenFactory[3], brings those steps together. For this change, it is fair to ask whether a developer needs to write each line.

Robert C. Martin, better known as Uncle Bob, told Aviator[2] that when he works with agents, he pays closer attention to architecture and how the software behaves. For the booking, that would mean looking at what the two channels share. If the app and the dashboard decide separately when to issue a refund, the next duplicate may come through a different route.

Will AI replace software developers? Some changes may take less hands-on programming. Team size is a separate question. In 2025, the ILO and Poland’s NASK institute[4] estimated that about one in four workers worldwide held a job with some exposure to generative AI. The estimate concerns tasks that might change. In our booking example, the company can use the time it saves to investigate problems like this one, or use the savings to shrink the team. That choice belongs to the company.

Who gets the time AI saves?

While the fix awaits approval, someone in finance reconciles the payments. Support has to explain the duplicate refund to the customer. In Atlas of AI[5], Kate Crawford follows the work that happens out of sight, along with the energy and materials these systems depend on. Apply that shift in perspective here. The engineering report records a completed ticket. Where does the hour support spent sorting it out show up?

When a compliment becomes a target

In Discipline and Punish, Michel Foucault describes how discipline organizes time and movement. In Capital, Karl Marx considers what happens when machines set the pace. Bruna Brito and I discussed how platforms shape behavior in an essay on social media for MIT Technology Review Brasil[6]. In teams that work with agents, watch what happens when the dashboard rewards closed tickets. Talking to support before making the change can look like a delay. Until the first call comes in.

“You’re a machine” used to be a compliment for someone who handled everything without slowing down. Now that the machine can close the ticket, who gets credit for being the first to say it’s still open?
